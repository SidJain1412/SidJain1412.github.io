Engineers can now ship faster than ever, thanks to coding agents.

There were 1 billion commits to GitHub in 2025. Now it’s 275 million per week.

We are building more than ever before.

But here’s an uncomfortable question:

_**How much of what we build actually gets used?**_

We’ve gotten very good at building things.
Not so good at building things people care about.

## The Illusion of Progress

When you can ship faster, it *feels* like you’re making progress.
More commits. More features. More releases.

But shipping is not the same as delivering value.

You can ship 10 features and have none of them move the needle, or ship one thing that actually matters.

The problem is, with coding agents, the cost of building has dropped so much that we don’t feel this anymore.
So we just keep building.

## Side projects

You, the person reading this, have probably vibe-coded a few projects in the past year.

I have too.

They’re fun. You learn a lot. You ship something in a weekend that would’ve taken months before.

_But how many of these projects solve a real problem, or have even 10 monthly active users?_

Very few, and that’s fine! Getting users isn't always the point of side projects.

But they reveal something important:

It’s very easy to build something.  
It’s very hard to build something people actually want.

## Enterprises: where this gets expensive

The same behavior is of course naturally leaking into real products with real, paying customers.
When building becomes easy, validation starts to feel optional.

Engineers see something cool, spin up a feature, and want to ship it, without
- talking to PMs
- involving design
- understanding customer pain
- checking with customer success or sales

I'm guilty of doing this more times than I can count, and it's not because we don’t care, but because building is just... easier and more fun.

And now, much faster.

The result is often a feature that no one uses, products full of half-adopted and half-complete ideas, and projects stuck in beta limbo forever

## What coding agents don’t fix

AI has made engineering faster, no doubt about that, but it only speeds up one part of the process: writing code.

It doesn’t help you decide whether the code should exist in the first place.

It won’t tell you if a user actually needs this.
It won’t tell you why something isn’t being adopted.
It won’t sit in a call and notice confusion, or hear what people actually mean when they describe a problem.

We’ve compressed the time it takes to build something.

We haven’t compressed the time it takes for someone to trust it, adopt it, and rely on it.

That part is still slow, and it will almost always be slower than building.

## The bottleneck is no longer engineering

It's the slow, boring work - talking to users, sitting with their feedback, waiting for adoption. The parts most engineers (including me) actively avoid.

Because building the next thing is just more fun!

## So what do you do?

If you don’t want to spend $500 in Claude credits building something no one uses, get involved in the full lifecycle.

- Talk to users (or at least listen to those who do!)
- Follow your features after they ship.
- Work with PMs, design, customer success. Go on calls with customers if you get the chance.
- Care about adoption, not just output.

Your job shouldn't end when the code hits production, that’s when it actually starts.

In a world where everyone can build, building is no longer the differentiator. What matters is whether anyone cares.

And that part hasn’t gotten any faster.
